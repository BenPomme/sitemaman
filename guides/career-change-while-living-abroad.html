Career change while living abroad: separate the role from the country first

Home Resources Career change while living abroad: separate the role from the country first

Published 11 October 2026 Updated 11 October 2026 Published by the Sylviane Bahr website

Lire ce guide en français

Making a career decision when you already live abroad fuses two questions that do not have the same answer: the question of the role and the question of the country. While they stay glued together, no option really becomes clear.

This guide keeps them apart, then gives a four-block decision sheet and concrete phrases for talking it through with a partner, a manager or a parent.

Two decisions that look like one

When a professional choice comes up abroad, people often frame it as a single question: do I stay here? Two separate questions hide behind it. The first is about the work itself: the role, the content, how decisions get made, the energy it takes. The second is about the country: where you want to live, with whom, and what you are willing to build the next stretch on.

Mixing them produces fog. An interesting role can make a country bearable, and a bad role can make a country look wrong. The reverse also happens: a good quality of life can hide the fact that you have been bored at work for a year.

First useful move: write the two decisions at the top of a page under two headings. It does not solve anything yet, but it separates what you decide from what you undergo.

  • Role question: what I do, with whom, at what pace, with what recognition.
  • Country question: where I want to live, with whom, and for how long.
  • Link question: how much does one depend on the other in my situation.

What adjustment research says, and what it does not

Research on self-initiated expatriates mostly studies how people adapt in a new country, and several studies link that adjustment to contextual factors, including the work setting. A survey of 125 self-initiated expatriates observed that, among several types of organisational culture, clan culture (collaborative, supportive) was associated with better adjustment, at work and outside work. That is an association observed in a group, neither a cause nor a guarantee for one person.

A qualitative study interviewed twenty direct supervisors who themselves had international experience. They describe perceiving their experience as useful and focusing their support on the work setting rather than on life outside work. These are the supervisors' reported perceptions, not a measurement of how the newcomers adjusted.

What this work does not say: it does not predict whether a specific role will suit you, it does not replace a personal decision, and it describes groups, not your story. Treat it as a set of bearings, not an answer.

Four questions that surface what you are really optimising for

Before comparing options, it helps to know what you are actually looking for. These four questions are not answered in a minute. Take time to write them, because an honest answer makes everything after it simpler.

  • If everything went as well as possible in two years, what would an ordinary week look like? Describe the daily reality, not a job title.
  • What am I willing to give up, even if it costs me: status, security, family proximity, salary, the language I work in?
  • How much of my dissatisfaction comes from the work itself, and how much comes from life outside work?
  • If I stayed here one more year without changing role, what would it really cost me, and what would it give me?

A decision sheet in four blocks

Once the two decisions are separate, options can be compared on a stable base. Sort what you know into four blocks, then judge each option block by block instead of as a whole.

The constraints column matters as much as the others. Social security, tax and insurance rules can vary with the country, your residence and your situation. The European Union states that these consequences exist and have to be checked with the competent bodies before you decide. That is one more reason not to settle everything at the same moment.

  • Work conditions: role content, autonomy, load, hours, quality of management, realistic progression over two years.
  • Values: what work has to make possible (contribution, learning, stability, status, impact), and what makes it unacceptable.
  • Country and legal constraints: permits, mobility, social security, tax, how your experience is recognised if you return, conditions for a partner or children.
  • Personal wellbeing: sleep, energy, health, relationships, time outside work, and the sense of moving in a direction that fits you.

How to talk about it with a partner, a manager or a parent

The same plan often runs into two sources of resistance, your own and other people's. It shows when a partner also gains something from staying, or when a manager hears a resignation where you were raising a question. The problem is not disagreement, it is discovering it too late, after deciding alone.

A few opening lines help move out of position against position, back to the shared problem.

  • To a partner: I am not deciding alone, I want to put what worries me on the table so we can look at it together, without solving it tonight.
  • To a manager: I need to clarify what is realistic in my role over the next twelve months, before I look further ahead.
  • To a parent: I am gathering facts to decide, not announcing a decision, and I would like your view on the part that concerns you.

Therapy, coaching, or neither

Once the facts are laid out, a less rational part often remains: fear of losing what you have built, guilt at not feeling grateful, tiredness at starting again. This is where the frame matters more than the topic.

A structured coaching frame fits when the question is to clarify a decision, turn it into steps, and work on a professional posture. Therapy fits more when what blocks you is emotional, older, or tied to patterns that repeat from one country to the next. If you are hesitating between the two, the compare page sets out the difference in a short read.

Neither is compulsory. Sometimes the right first step is an information conversation, with someone who knows both international mobility and decision work.

Exercise: the decision sheet

Take two sheets of paper. On the first, write the four questions. On the second, draw a table with one column per option and four rows, one per block.

  1. Answer the four questions first. Leave an imperfect answer if that is what you have today, it will sharpen.
  2. Fill one column per serious option (stay, change role, return, move elsewhere) and the four rows: work conditions, values, country and legal constraints, personal wellbeing.
  3. In each cell, write a checkable fact or the worry that needs checking. Separate explicitly what you know from what you assume.
  4. Mark the cells where you need outside information (contract, tax, permits, school) and note who can provide it.
  5. Write one opening line for each person concerned, before you say it to them.
  6. Set a review date three weeks out. A decision rarely improves when taken in a single evening.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How long should the decision take?

There is no right duration. A structuring decision often takes several weeks, with back and forth. Setting a review date helps more than aiming for an immediate answer.

Do I have to leave everything for a new project?

Rarely all at once. You can start with a test: a conversation, a parallel project, a trial period. You keep some ground under you until the new direction is solid.

My partner does not want to leave, what now?

Start from the shared problem, not the decision. What each of you risks losing counts as much as what each of you wants to gain. An information conversation can help set out the terms before you choose.

Do I need a coach or a therapist?

It depends on what is blocking. To clarify a decision and build steps, a coaching frame fits. For an emotional or older block, therapy is more suitable. The difference is set out on the Therapy or coaching page.

Sources and references

Sources are listed for transparency.

  • European Union, Working abroad (Your Europe) · Official source to state that the consequences of a job in another country touch benefits, tax and insurance, and that the rules depend on the situation and the country. To be checked case by case, never generalised.
  • Baneviciene et al., 2025, PLOS ONE · Qualitative study: twenty semi-structured interviews with direct supervisors who themselves had international experience. These supervisors describe perceiving their experience as useful and focusing their support on the work setting rather than on life outside work. These are reported perceptions, not a causal measurement of expatriates' adjustment.
  • Kumpikaite-Valiuniene et al., 2022, Frontiers in Psychology · Survey of 125 self-initiated expatriates: among four organisational culture types studied, clan culture (collaborative, supportive) was associated with better work and non-work adjustment.
