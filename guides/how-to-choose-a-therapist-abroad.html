How to choose a therapist abroad: the questions to ask before you start

Home Resources How to choose a therapist abroad: the questions to ask before you start

Published 11 October 2026 Updated 11 October 2026 Published by the Sylviane Bahr website

Lire ce guide en français

Looking for support in a country that is not yours adds unknowns to an already personal task: the working language, how qualifications are recognised, fees, confidentiality, and what happens if you move again.

This guide gathers twelve questions to ask before choosing, a way to look at the language question without promising that a first language is always better, and six questions you can settle on your own.

Clarify what you are looking for before you look

Most people who start searching know they want to feel better, but not what shape that should take. Separating two or three needs avoids comparing offers that do not answer the same request.

A simple map is enough: understanding what repeats, getting through a specific period, or finding a direction in life or work. You do not have to pick a diagnosis, only a starting point for the conversation.

  • Understand: make sense of a period, a pattern, an emotion that keeps returning.
  • Get through: hold steady during a transition, a grief, a separation, a relocation.
  • Direct: clarify a life or work decision within a more structured frame.

Twelve questions to ask before you start

These questions have no single answer. The answers help you assess qualifications, the agreed frame and the fit, and they need to be clear. You can ask them of several practitioners.

  • 1. Which language will sessions be in, and can that change along the way?
  • 2. What is your training, and how can I check the qualifications or certifications you mention?
  • 3. In which country and under which framework do you practise, and what does that imply for me where I live?
  • 4. How is the session fee set, and is it confirmed before I start?
  • 5. What are the cancellation terms, and is there a charge if I need to move a session?
  • 6. How often will sessions take place, and how long are they?
  • 7. What happens between sessions if I need contact?
  • 8. How is confidentiality handled, and in which cases is there an exception?
  • 9. Do you work online, and what happens if I move during our work together?
  • 10. What happens in an emergency or crisis, and who do I contact then?
  • 11. How many sessions before we review the direction of the work?
  • 12. How will I know that this frame, or this practitioner, is not right for me?

The language question, handled without exaggeration

The working language matters, but it is often presented as a single criterion when it is one variable among others. Caldwell-Harris suggests that emotional content is experienced with a different intensity depending on whether you speak in your first language or in a language learned later.

A narrative review focused on refugees indicates that a first language can make it easier to put certain experiences into words. The review also names risks, such as over-identification or a therapeutic mismatch. It is not a study conducted on patients, and that population is not the same as internationally mobile professionals, so these points do not transfer as they stand.

Practical consequence: it is not established that working in your first language is always better. What matters is that the chosen language lets you say what is important, with a person and a frame that hold. For some, that will be the original language. For others, a learned language that usefully keeps a little distance.

The order of the steps, and what each step is not

A search happens in three moves. The first is sorting: a few practitioners, a few questions, a comparison. The second is an information conversation, to see whether you click and whether the frame is realistic. The third is the first working session.

The information conversation is not a therapy session. In this practice the first conversation lasts around thirty minutes, it is free, and it is there to lay out what brings you here, to ask your questions and to decide together whether continuing makes sense. It commits you to nothing, and with another practitioner this first conversation can take a different shape.

For online work, one useful precision concerns where you are when sessions happen. Availability depends on your current location, the framework that applies there, the fit, and whether video work is suitable. That is confirmed before you start, not after.

Therapy, coaching or care: do not confuse the frames

These frames overlap more than they are usually made to. Therapy works on what repeats and what is emotionally loaded. Coaching works on a goal and steps, usually professional. And a doctor or a qualified mental-health clinician can assess symptoms and offer care, including talking therapies. They are not three separate worlds.

Sorting needs by whether a symptom is physical or emotional is not enough, because the two are often linked. What helps is looking at the qualifications and the frame of the person you consult, and at what you need. If you are unsure, if distress persists, or if the situation becomes acute, a doctor or a qualified mental-health professional can help you find the right support. The compare page sets out only the difference between therapy and coaching, in a short read.

This guide and this site do not present themselves as a medical, psychiatric or emergency service. If you are in immediate danger, contact your country's emergency services.

Exercise: six questions to answer on your own

Before contacting anyone, answer these six questions on your own. They turn a vague search into a list of criteria you can check.

  1. Which language do I want to work in, and what am I willing to do if no available practitioner works in it?
  2. What is my maximum monthly budget for this work, counting the real frequency and not only the fee for one session?
  3. Am I mainly looking for understanding, for getting through a period, or for a clear direction?
  4. Do I want in-person, online, or both, and why?
  5. Where will I physically be in the next three to six months, and does that change my choice?
  6. How will I know, after a few sessions, that this frame suits me or does not?
  7. Only then, write down the three questions from the list of twelve that matter most to you, and ask them of every practitioner you contact.

Frequently asked questions

Frequently asked questions

How do I know whether a practitioner suits me?

The first signal is simple: you feel understood without having to explain everything twice. The second is that the frame is clear (duration, fee, frequency, confidentiality). Both can be checked from the information conversation, with no commitment.

Do I have to work in my first language?

No. That is not established as a rule. For some people the first language makes certain nuances more accessible. For others, a learned language provides a useful distance. The right criterion is what lets you say what matters, within a frame that holds.

What if I move again during the work?

Ask before you start. Availability depends on your location when sessions happen and on the framework that applies there, so online work is not guaranteed. That is confirmed before you begin.

Can I change my mind once I have started?

Yes. Support gets reviewed. If the frame does not suit you, saying so is part of the work. Nothing obliges you to continue, and the question can be raised without it calling your whole approach into question.
